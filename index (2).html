Sharia vs common law inheritance
What's the difference, and which applies to you
›

In Nigeria, which inheritance rules apply to you often depends on how you married and where you live, not just your faith. If you married under the Marriage Act (a court or registry wedding), your estate is generally distributed under the Administration of Estates Law, with fixed shares for spouse and children. If you married under Islamic law (nikah) and did not also register under the Marriage Act, sharia inheritance rules typically apply, especially in states with sharia courts, and shares follow the fixed portions set out in the Quran for spouses, children, and other relatives.

Where things get complicated: a person can have a valid nikah and still be treated as married under common law in some circumstances, and the presence of a will changes what a court will recognise, sometimes even overriding intestate rules depending on the estate and the beneficiaries. This is exactly the kind of situation where the "right" answer depends on your specific documents and location, not a general rule.

Marriage & divorce, two systems
Nikah, court registration, and dissolution
›

A nikah (Islamic marriage contract) is recognised under sharia law but is not automatically registered with the Nigerian government. If you want your marriage recognised under common law too, for things like inheritance, immigration, or joint property, it needs to be separately registered under the Marriage Act, usually at a registry or through a licensed venue.

Dissolution works differently across the two systems. Under sharia, divorce can happen through talaq (pronounced by the husband), khul (initiated by the wife, often with return of dowry), or through a sharia court ruling. Under common law, dissolving a registered marriage requires a court process through the High Court, typically citing grounds such as unreasonable behaviour or a period of separation. If your marriage exists under both systems, both processes may need to be addressed to fully close the matter.

Child custody after divorce
What courts weigh, in both systems
›

Under common law, custody decisions are guided primarily by the "best interest of the child", covering the child's age, welfare, education, and stability, rather than automatically favouring either parent. Courts can also order joint custody, with one parent having primary physical custody and the other having visitation rights.

Under sharia law, custody (hadanah) commonly follows age-based guidelines, with young children often placed with the mother and older children's placement considered more flexibly, though this varies by school of thought and specific circumstances. In both systems, an existing agreement between parents can guide the outcome, but a court retains the final say if there's a dispute.

Prenuptial and marriage contracts
Protecting yourself before you marry
›

A prenuptial agreement sets out how assets, debts, and property would be divided if a marriage ends, and Nigerian courts increasingly recognise them, though they aren't automatically binding the way they are in some other countries, a court can still review one for fairness.

In Islamic marriage, conditions can be written directly into the nikah contract itself, covering things like the right to work, education, where the couple will live, or conditions around polygamy. Both routes exist to reduce uncertainty later, and both are far more effective when drafted properly rather than as an informal understanding.

Domestic violence: your legal options
Protection, reporting, and next steps
›

Domestic violence is a criminal matter, not just a private family issue, and several Nigerian states now have specific laws (such as Violence Against Persons Prohibition laws) allowing for protection orders that legally require an abuser to stay away. A police report and medical documentation of any injury both strengthen a case significantly.

You don't have to choose between reporting and staying safe, protection orders exist specifically to address that gap, and organisations and shelters exist in most states to support immediate safety needs alongside the legal process.

If you're in immediate danger, contact the police first, then reach out for legal support.

Tenancy agreements, explained
Your rights before you sign anything
›

A tenancy agreement should clearly state the rent amount, payment schedule, duration, notice period required before eviction, and who is responsible for repairs. Verbal agreements are legally recognised in Nigeria but are much harder to enforce if a dispute arises, so a written agreement genuinely protects both sides.

Landlords generally cannot evict a tenant without proper notice, and the required notice period depends on the type of tenancy (monthly, yearly) and state tenancy law. A landlord locking you out or seizing property without a court order is typically unlawful, regardless of whether rent is owed.

Buying land safely
Title checks before you pay anyone
›

Before paying for land, verify the title at the relevant state lands registry, a Certificate of Occupancy or governor's consent should exist or be transferable, and a search will show if the land is genuinely free of disputes, government acquisition, or multiple claims. Never rely solely on what the seller or local agent tells you.

Family-owned land ("omonile" situations in some areas) is a common source of dispute, since one family member may sell land that others also have a claim to. A proper deed of assignment, correctly witnessed and later registered, is what actually protects your ownership, not just receipts or a handshake agreement.

Considering a purchase? A pre-purchase legal review costs far less than a dispute later.

Certificate of Occupancy, explained
What it actually proves
›

A Certificate of Occupancy (C of O) is issued by a state government and grants the holder a right of occupancy over land, it is the strongest form of title recognition available since all land in Nigeria is technically vested in the state governor under the Land Use Act. It is not the same as outright ownership in the way some people assume, but it is what courts and buyers rely on most heavily.

A C of O can be transferred through a proper deed of assignment with governor's consent, and buying land without one, or without a clear plan to obtain one, carries real risk of a later dispute over legitimacy.

Landlord-tenant disputes: what to do
Before it escalates to court
›

Most disputes, unpaid rent, disagreements over repairs, disputes about deposit refunds, start with a written notice stating the issue clearly and giving a reasonable timeframe to resolve it. This creates a paper trail that matters significantly if the dispute later goes to court.

If informal resolution fails, tenancy matters typically go through the magistrate court in the relevant local jurisdiction, and a landlord cannot lawfully change locks, remove belongings, or cut off utilities to force a tenant out, this is illegal self-help eviction regardless of how much rent is owed.

Family land and inheritance disputes
When multiple relatives claim the same land
›

Family land disputes are among the most common and most drawn-out property cases, often involving a piece of land inherited across generations where documentation was never properly updated, or where one branch of a family sells without the consent of others who also have a legitimate claim.

Resolving these usually starts with establishing the actual chain of ownership and who the rightful successors are, sometimes through customary or sharia inheritance principles depending on the family's practice, before any sale or subdivision can be considered valid. Acting early, before a sale happens, is far easier than reversing one after the fact.

Registering your business
CAC, structure, and what it protects
›

Every business operating formally in Nigeria should be registered with the Corporate Affairs Commission (CAC), either as a Business Name (simpler, for sole proprietors) or a Limited Liability Company (more paperwork, but it separates your personal assets from business debts and liabilities).

Beyond CAC, you'll typically need a Tax Identification Number (TIN), and depending on your sector, additional licenses or permits. Choosing the right structure early affects your tax obligations, your personal liability if the business runs into debt, and how easily you can raise investment or take on partners later.

What makes a contract enforceable
Beyond just signing something
›

A contract needs an offer, acceptance of that offer, something of value exchanged (consideration), and a genuine intention by both sides to be legally bound. A contract doesn't have to be in writing to be enforceable in Nigeria, but written contracts are far easier to prove and enforce if a dispute arises.

Common mistakes: vague terms that leave room for disagreement, no clear timeline or deliverables, missing signatures or dates, and no clause covering what happens if one side doesn't perform. A well-drafted contract protects you before a problem happens, not after.

Partnership agreements: what to include
Avoiding disputes before they start
›

A solid partnership agreement should clearly state each partner's capital contribution, ownership percentage, profit and loss sharing, decision-making authority, and what happens if one partner wants to exit, is pushed out, or the business is dissolved. Verbal partnerships based on trust are exactly where the most painful disputes tend to start.

It's also worth addressing intellectual property ownership (who owns what's built), non-compete expectations, and a clear dispute resolution process, mediation or arbitration first, before either party heads to court. These clauses feel unnecessary until the moment they aren't.

Debt recovery: getting what you're owed
Formal steps that actually work
›

The first formal step is usually a lawyer's demand letter, a written notice giving the debtor a set period to pay before further action, and it's surprisingly effective on its own since it signals the matter is now serious. If that doesn't work, recovery can proceed through the small claims court (for smaller amounts, a faster process) or the regular civil court process for larger sums.

Keeping clear records, invoices, written agreements, communication trails, makes a debt recovery case significantly stronger and faster to resolve.

Employment contracts and your rights
For employers and employees
›

A proper employment contract should cover role, salary, working hours, leave entitlement, termination notice period, and grounds for dismissal. Nigerian labour law requires written terms of employment to be provided within a reasonable period of starting work, and disputes are far harder to resolve fairly without this documentation in place.

Wrongful termination, unpaid entitlements, and unclear severance terms are among the most common disputes on both sides, employer and employee. Whether you're hiring or being hired, having the contract properly reviewed before signing protects you either way.

Your rights if arrested
What the police can and can't do
›

If arrested, you have the right to be told the reason for your arrest, the right to remain silent beyond identifying yourself, and the right to contact a lawyer or a family member. You cannot lawfully be held indefinitely without charge, Nigerian law requires you to be brought before a court within a reasonable time, generally within 24 to 48 hours depending on the circumstances and how far the court is.

Torture, assault, or inhuman treatment in custody is illegal regardless of the alleged offence. If you or someone you know is arrested, the priority is getting a lawyer involved as early as possible.

Understanding bail
It's a right, not a favour
›

Bail is not an admission of guilt, it simply allows you to remain free while your case proceeds. For most offences, bail is a right, either granted directly by the police for minor matters or by a court for more serious ones, often with conditions like a surety who guarantees your return.

It is illegal for police to demand payment directly in exchange for bail, this is a well-known abuse, not a legitimate process, "bail is free" is the standard reminder for a reason. Some serious offences carry restricted or conditional bail, which is where legal representation becomes especially important.

What happens at a police station
Knowing the process reduces the fear
›

After an arrest, you'll typically be taken to a station, have your details recorded, and be asked to make a statement. You are not obligated to say anything beyond your identity without a lawyer present, and any statement you do give can be used later, so it's worth being deliberate rather than answering out of pressure or fear.

You have the right to know what you're being charged with, and being held without any charge for an extended period is not lawful. Requesting a lawyer, and having someone informed of where you are, are both reasonable and legal requests at any point.

Criminal record and expungement
Whether a past charge can be cleared
›

A criminal record can affect employment, travel, and licensing long after a case is closed, and whether it can be cleared depends heavily on the outcome, an acquittal, a discharge, or a conviction each carry different implications and different paths forward. Nigeria doesn't have one single, simple expungement process the way some other countries do, so this is very case-specific.

In some cases, a formal application to the court that handled the matter, or a presidential pardon route for older convictions, may be relevant depending on the offence and outcome.

Reporting a crime: what to expect
As a victim or witness
›

Reporting starts with a written statement at the police station, and it helps significantly to bring any evidence you have, photos, messages, witness contacts, at the time of reporting rather than later. You're entitled to a copy of your statement and the case reference number.

If a report isn't being taken seriously, or investigation seems to have stalled, a lawyer can help push the process along, follow up formally, or advise on a private prosecution route in genuinely serious cases where that's applicable.

Which court handles this?
Sharia, customary, magistrate, or high court
›

Sharia courts exist mainly in northern states and handle matters between Muslims where sharia law applies, commonly marriage, divorce, and inheritance.

Customary courts handle matters governed by local or ethnic custom, often overlapping with family and land matters in areas where customary law is recognised.

Magistrate courts handle smaller civil claims, minor criminal matters, and tenancy disputes up to a certain value, varying by state.

High courts handle more serious civil and criminal matters, larger claims, constitutional questions, and appeals from magistrate, customary, or sharia courts in many states.

How long a civil case takes
Setting realistic expectations
›

Timelines vary widely. Small claims or magistrate court matters can resolve in months, while High Court cases commonly take one to three years, sometimes longer with appeals, depending on court backlog, the complexity of evidence, and how cooperative the other side is.

Settlement or negotiation can shorten this significantly, and is often worth exploring before committing to a full trial timeline, though it isn't always the right move depending on what's at stake.

Mediation and arbitration explained
Alternatives to a full court case
›

Mediation involves a neutral third party helping both sides reach a voluntary agreement, it's faster, cheaper, and more private than court, though the outcome isn't binding unless both sides agree to it. Arbitration is more formal, the arbitrator's decision is typically binding, and it's commonly used for business and commercial disputes, especially where a contract already specifies arbitration as the resolution method.

Both routes can resolve a dispute in weeks or months rather than years, and are worth exploring before assuming court is the only option.

Small claims: recovering money owed
A faster, simpler court process
›

Small claims courts exist in many states specifically to handle debt and money disputes below a certain value quickly, often without needing a lawyer to file, though having one still strengthens your case. The process is generally faster and less formal than a full civil trial, with judgment often reached within weeks to a few months.

Clear documentation, invoices, agreements, proof of the debt, is what makes or breaks a small claims case, since the process moves quickly and doesn't allow much room for prolonged evidence-gathering.

Appealing a court decision
What it takes, and what it doesn't fix
›

An appeal challenges how the law was applied or how the process was conducted, not simply the fact that you disagree with the outcome, so it needs a genuine legal ground, an error in law, procedure, or evidence handling, to succeed. Appeals also have strict filing deadlines, often just weeks from the original judgment, so acting quickly matters.

Appeals move through a higher court (from magistrate to High Court, or High Court to Court of Appeal) and can themselves take a significant amount of time, so it's worth weighing the strength of the grounds against the cost and time involved before proceeding.
