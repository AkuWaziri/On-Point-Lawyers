A widow's rights after her husband's death
Protecting yourself from being pushed out
›

Widows in Nigeria are sometimes pressured by in-laws to leave the matrimonial home or sign away property soon after a husband's death, this is not lawful, and a widow retains rights to her share of the estate under whichever system applied to the marriage, sharia or common law, regardless of family pressure.

Acting quickly to secure documents, property records, and evidence of the marriage protects your position significantly, delays make it easier for others to move assets or dispute your claim.

When fatherhood is contested
›

Paternity disputes commonly arise around child maintenance, inheritance, or custody, and Nigerian courts can order DNA testing to resolve the question where it's genuinely contested. Under sharia, legitimacy is generally tied to a valid marriage at the time of birth, which affects inheritance rights differently than under common law.

A child's rights to maintenance are not always dependent on the parents' marital status, this is worth understanding clearly rather than assuming disputed paternity ends financial responsibility.

Marriage registration for returning Nigerians

A marriage conducted abroad is generally recognised in Nigeria if it was valid where it took place, but for local matters, property, inheritance, immigration for a spouse, it's often necessary to register or formally document it locally as well, particularly if the original marriage certificate isn't in a form Nigerian institutions readily accept.

This becomes especially relevant when relocating back to Nigeria permanently, worth sorting out proactively rather than when a dispute or urgent need arises.

Cohabitation without marriage
What rights unmarried partners actually have
›

Property acquired jointly can still be claimed based on actual financial contribution, but this needs to be proven, which is exactly why documentation matters even in an unmarried relationship involving shared assets.

Remedies for breach of contract
What you can actually claim
›

When the other side breaches a contract, common remedies include damages (financial compensation for your loss), specific performance (a court order forcing them to fulfil the contract), or rescission (cancelling the contract entirely), which remedy fits depends heavily on what the contract says and what you actually lost.

Courts generally compensate for actual proven loss, not simply what you hoped to gain, keeping records of your losses strengthens any claim significantly.

Theft and stealing charges
What separates the two, and what to expect
›

Nigerian criminal law distinguishes stealing (taking property with intent to permanently deprive the owner) from related offences like obtaining by false pretences (fraud), the specific charge affects both the potential penalty and the defence strategy, so the exact wording of a charge matters more than people often assume.

Being accused doesn't mean conviction is automatic, the prosecution must prove intent, not just possession or access, which is often the strongest point in a proper defence.

Defamation: civil claim or criminal charge
Protecting your reputation the right way
›

Defamation in Nigeria can be pursued as a civil claim for damages, or in some cases as a criminal charge for criminal libel, though criminal defamation prosecutions are less common and generally reserved for more serious cases. A civil claim requires showing the statement was false, published to a third party, and caused harm to your reputation.

Screenshots and evidence of publication, who saw it, when, and the actual harm caused, are essential to building a strong defamation case.

Drug offences and penalties
What the law actually covers
›

Drug offences in Nigeria are handled primarily under the NDLEA Act, and penalties vary significantly depending on the substance, quantity, and whether the charge is possession versus trafficking, a distinction that dramatically changes both the process and potential sentence. Being present where drugs are found doesn't automatically establish possession in law.

Early legal representation matters enormously in drug cases, procedural errors in how evidence was obtained are a common and legitimate line of defence.

Traffic offences and your rights

Vehicle impoundment must follow proper procedure too, an officer generally cannot simply drive off with your vehicle without documentation of why.

Marriage annulment vs divorce
A different process, with different effects
›

An annulment declares a marriage was never legally valid in the first place, grounds include lack of consent, existing prior marriage, or fraud, while divorce ends a marriage that was validly formed. The distinction matters for inheritance, remarriage timing, and how property is treated afterward.

Annulment is harder to obtain than divorce since it requires proving the marriage was defective from the start, not simply that it later broke down.

Compulsory acquisition and compensation
When government takes your land

Government can compulsorily acquire private land for public purposes under the Land Use Act, but this requires proper notice and, in most cases, fair compensation to the affected owner. Compensation amounts are sometimes disputed, and owners have the right to challenge an unfairly low valuation before an appropriate tribunal or court.

Joint tenancy vs tenancy in common
Two very different ways to co-own property
›

Under joint tenancy, co-owners have equal, undivided shares, and if one dies, their share automatically passes to the surviving owner(s), not through their will. Under tenancy in common, each owner holds a distinct, separately inheritable share, which can be unequal and passes according to their will or inheritance rules, not automatically to co-owners.

Which structure applies significantly affects what happens to the property when an owner dies or wants to sell their share, worth confirming clearly at the point of purchase.

Lease vs tenancy: what's the difference
Terms that affect your rights significantly
›

A lease is generally a longer-term arrangement (often years), offering more formal rights and sometimes registrable interests, while a tenancy is typically shorter-term (monthly or yearly) with more flexible, less formal terms. Notice periods, renewal rights, and what happens at the end of the term differ significantly between the two.

Calling an arrangement a "lease" in conversation doesn't make it one legally, what matters is the actual duration and terms agreed, worth checking your document carefully.

›

Nigeria's Child Rights Act and related state laws treat offenders under 18 differently from adults, generally through juvenile courts focused more on rehabilitation than punishment, with different detention conditions and processes. Self-defence: what the law allows
When force is legally justified
›

Nigerian law recognises a right to self-defence, but the force used must be reasonable and proportionate to the threat faced, using significantly more force than necessary can itself become a criminal matter, even if you were initially the one attacked. Context, immediacy of the threat, and available alternatives all factor into how a court assesses this.

If you've acted in self-defence and now face charges, documenting the circumstances and any witnesses as early as possible strengthens your position considerably.
